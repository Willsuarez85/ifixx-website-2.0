The best kitchen cabinet materials for most remodels are a plywood box where water is close, MDF doors if you want paint, and solid wood or wood veneer doors if you want a wood tone. The tier you buy, RTA, semi-custom or custom, usually moves the price more than the material does.

Most cabinet quotes hide that in a few words: "furniture board", "all plywood construction", "MDF doors". If you can't tell which part of the cabinet each one describes, you can't tell if the upgrade is worth paying for.

We remodel kitchens. We're not a cabinet showroom, so we look at cabinets as one line in the whole budget, next to the countertops, the floor and the labor.

This guide covers new cabinets going into a remodel: what each part should be made of, what each tier costs, where you can save and what to ask for in writing.

## The three parts of a cabinet, and why each one needs a different material

A cabinet is three things: the box, the door and the drawer. Each one takes a different kind of abuse, so each one has a different best material. Treat them as three decisions.

| Part | Best value pick | Why | Where it matters most |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Box** (sides, floor, shelves) | Plywood | Holds screws well and handles a small leak better than particleboard or MDF | Sink base and next to the dishwasher |
| **Painted door** | MDF, or a wood frame with an MDF center panel | No grain and very little movement, so paint stays smooth | Every painted door |
| **Wood-tone door** | Solid wood, or wood veneer over plywood | Stain needs real grain | Stained and natural finishes |
| **Drawers and hardware** | Solid wood drawer box, dovetail joints, full-extension soft-close slides | These are the parts you use every day | Every base cabinet |

Read your quote with that table next to it. If the quote names one material for "the cabinets", ask which part it means. Often it describes the door, and the box is something else.

## Plywood vs. MDF vs. particleboard: what's inside the box

The box is the part you never see and the part that decides how long the kitchen lasts. Three materials cover almost every cabinet sold today.

- **Plywood.** Thin layers of wood glued with the grain crossed. It's strong for its weight, holds screws at the hinges and slides, and survives a small leak better than the other two. It also costs the most.
- **MDF.** Wood fiber pressed into a dense, smooth panel. It's flat, stable and takes paint very well. It's heavy, and an unsealed edge soaks up water.
- **Particleboard.** Wood chips and resin, often sold as "furniture board". It's the cheapest and it stays flat. Once it gets wet it swells and doesn't come back.

Here's the honest part: particleboard isn't automatically bad. A good 3/4-inch particleboard box in a wall cabinet or a pantry, far from water, can serve you well. A thin, cheap plywood isn't automatically good either.

So the question is where the box goes and how thick it is. [This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/kitchens/all-about-cabinets) says stock cabinets usually have 1/2-inch MDF or particleboard boxes, semi-custom typically use 1/2-inch MDF with a plywood upgrade, and custom typically use 3/4-inch plywood.

Ask for the box thickness before you ask for the brand. It tells you more.

### What each material costs

For a national reference, [Fixr's cabinet installation cost guide](https://www.fixr.com/costs/cabinets-installation) (updated January 2026) lists these ranges per linear foot. They are national numbers, and Charlotte quotes will vary.

| Material | Cost per linear foot (Fixr, national) | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MDF** | $50 to $250 | Painted doors and panels | Unsealed edges near water |
| **Laminate** | $60 to $250 | Flat, modern doors on a budget | Chips are hard to repair |
| **Wood veneer** | $60 to $300 | The wood look for less | Thin veneer can't be sanded much |
| **Plywood** | $100 to $500 | Boxes, mostly near water | Thin or low-grade panels |
| **Solid wood** | $150 to $700 | Stained doors, face frames, drawer boxes | Moves with humidity; paint can crack at the joints |

## Solid wood, MDF or thermofoil doors: painted vs. stained

The door is where most people overspend. The rule is simple: decide the finish first, and the finish picks the material.

**If you want paint, MDF is your friend.** Solid wood swells and shrinks with the seasons, and on a painted door that movement shows up as hairline cracks where the frame pieces meet. MDF barely moves, so the paint stays clean.

A painted MDF door, or a wood frame with an MDF center panel, usually costs less than solid wood and looks better painted. If you're going to save somewhere, save here.

**If you want a wood tone, you need real grain.** That means solid wood doors, or wood veneer over plywood on flat panels and cabinet sides. Veneer gives you the same grain you'd see on solid wood for less money.

**Thermofoil** is a vinyl film heat-pressed onto MDF. It's inexpensive and easy to wipe down. Its weak point is heat.

[House Digest](https://www.housedigest.com/1930476/downsides-thermofoil-cabinet-doors-heat) explains that heat from the stove, and even steam from a long dishwasher cycle, can lead to melting, peeling, blistering and delamination. Heat shields between hot appliances and the cabinets help.

If thermofoil fits your budget, keep it away from the range and ask for heat shields next to the oven and the dishwasher.

## RTA, semi-custom or custom: what each tier costs and how long it takes

Material is half the story. The other half is the tier, and it moves the price more. All the numbers below are national, each with its source, so use them to compare tiers and not as a Charlotte quote.

| Tier | Per linear foot ([Fixr](https://www.fixr.com/costs/cabinets-installation)) | Whole kitchen ([Fine Homebuilding](https://www.finehomebuilding.com/2025/04/08/what-to-know-when-buying-kitchen-cabinetry)) | Typical box ([This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/kitchens/all-about-cabinets)) | Lead time ([Kitchen Cabinet Kings](https://kitchencabinetkings.com/blog/kitchen-cabinet-lead-times/)) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RTA or stock** | $100 to $300 (stock) | RTA from about $2,000; stock $6,000 to $8,000 | 1/2-inch MDF or particleboard | RTA 1 to 2 weeks; assembled 2 to 4 |
| **Semi-custom** | $150 to $650 | $20,000 to $30,000 | 1/2-inch MDF, plywood as an upgrade | 4 to 6 weeks |
| **Custom** | $500 to $1,200 | $35,000 to $50,000 and up | 3/4-inch plywood | 6 to 12 weeks |

A few notes on what those tiers mean in a real remodel:

- **RTA (ready-to-assemble)** ships flat and fast. Many RTA lines offer plywood boxes at a low price. Someone still has to build them, and Fine Homebuilding warns that assembling many RTA cabinets "turns out not to be a DIY job", so add that labor.
- **Stock** comes assembled in fixed sizes, usually in 3-inch steps. Fillers cover the gaps. It works well in a simple layout.
- **Semi-custom** adds sizes, finishes and upgrades. Watch the options list. Fine Homebuilding notes that the cost can "creep into the fully custom price range" once you add enough of them.
- **Custom** is built to your kitchen. It makes sense when the room has odd dimensions or you need something a catalog doesn't have.

Lead time matters as much as price. Published lead times vary from source to source and from line to line, so get the delivery date in writing.

## Where you can save without losing quality

You don't need the top of every column. These are the places where spending less costs you nothing you'll notice.

- **Painted MDF doors over solid wood.** Cheaper, and the better choice for paint.
- **Wood veneer over solid wood** on flat panels and cabinet sides when you want a wood tone.
- **Drop a tier before you drop the material.** A stock or RTA cabinet with a plywood box often beats a semi-custom one with a thin particleboard box.
- **Stick to standard sizes.** Every made-to-measure cabinet, extra filler and decorative panel adds to the bill.
- **One door style, one color.** A plain shaker door in a single finish is the least expensive door to make well.
- **Upgrade the box only where the water is.** If all-plywood doesn't fit the budget, ask if the sink base and the cabinets beside the dishwasher can be plywood on their own. Not every line allows it, so ask early.

## Where you should never cut corners

This is the part we don't cut corners on, and you shouldn't either.

![White shaker cabinets with brass pulls around an undermount sink and a stainless dishwasher, with a dark countertop and a gray tile backsplash](/images/projects/Ifixx-portfolio20.webp)

- **The sink base.** It sits under every connection in the kitchen. One slow drip inside a particleboard box and the floor of that cabinet swells. Make this one plywood.
- **Box thickness.** A 3/4-inch box holds hinges and slides better than a 1/2-inch one. Get the number in the quote.
- **Drawers and slides.** This Old House notes that dovetail joints stand up to constant abuse better than simpler joints, and that top-rated drawer slides are rated for up to 100 pounds.
- **Heat next to thermofoil.** No thermofoil beside the range, and heat shields where it meets an oven or a dishwasher.
- **The install.** Good cabinets hung out of level give you doors that don't line up and a countertop that needs shims. Ask who sets them and how they level the run.

## Does Charlotte humidity change the answer?

A little. Charlotte's relative humidity averages 67% over the day and 83% at 7 a.m., according to [Current Results](https://currentresults.com/Weather/North-Carolina/humidity-annual.php), which publishes 1961 to 1990 climate averages.

Inside an air-conditioned house the swing is smaller, but wood still moves between a damp summer and a dry, heated winter. Two things follow from that:

- **Painted solid wood doors are the ones that show it.** The joints open a hair in winter and the paint line cracks. It's cosmetic, and it's one more reason to paint MDF.
- **Water is a bigger risk than air.** Humidity alone won't ruin a decent particleboard box. A leak will. That's why the material near the sink matters more than the material in the pantry.

## What to ask for in writing before you sign

Two cabinet quotes can look the same and be very different kitchens. A much lower quote usually means a different box. Ask for it in writing:

- **Box material and thickness**, for wall and base cabinets.
- **The sink base material**, named on its own line.
- **Door and face frame material**, and if the center panel is MDF or wood.
- **Drawer box material and joint**: dovetail or stapled.
- **Slides and hinges**: full-extension, soft-close, and the brand.
- **The finish**: paint, stain or thermofoil, and how touch-ups are handled.
- **TSCA Title VI compliant panels.** The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/formaldehyde/formaldehyde-emission-standards-composite-wood-products) has required that label on hardwood plywood, MDF and particleboard since March 22, 2019, and the rule covers finished goods that contain them.
- **Lead time**, and who assembles the cabinets if they ship flat.
- **What the install includes**: fillers, end panels, crown, toe kicks and hardware.

If a seller won't put the box material in writing, that tells you something too.
