**Use epoxy when the rot is small, the wood is dry and the piece still holds its shape. Patch in new wood when a section is gone but most of the piece is sound, and replace the whole sill, trim board or deck board when the rot runs through it or reaches anything structural.**

That's the whole decision in three steps. The rest of this guide shows how to tell which step you're on, what each one costs, and why the water has to be fixed before any of them.

We repair rotten wood around Charlotte: window sills, exterior trim, door frames, deck boards, railings, posts and steps. We repair the wood around the window; we don't sell or replace windows. So we have no reason to push you toward a bigger job than you need.

## Why Wood Rots in Charlotte

Rot is a fungus, and it needs water. According to <a href="https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/powderpost-beetles-and-wood-inhabiting-fungi">NC State Extension</a>, decay fungi stop growing once wood moisture drops below 20%. Wood that stays soaked, around 30%, rots.

Charlotte gives those fungi plenty to work with. The <a href="https://www.weather.gov/media/gsp/Climate/CLT/CLTDECNORM.pdf">National Weather Service</a> puts normal rainfall at the airport at 43.6 inches a year, and <a href="https://www.currentresults.com/Yearly-Weather/USA/NC/Charlotte/recent-annual-charlotte-temperature-precipitation.php">recent years</a> have run wetter, with 59.2 inches in 2018.

Heat helps too. According to <a href="https://www.jlconline.com/deck-builder/what-causes-wood-to-rot_o">JLC</a>, decay fungi grow best between 70°F and 90°F, which describes most of a Charlotte summer.

Pressure-treated wood is not immune. The treatment doesn't reach the center of every board, so water that gets in through cracks, cut ends and screw holes can still rot it from the inside.

Rot almost always starts where water sits and the wood can't dry:

- The flat top of a window sill and the joint where it meets the side trim
- The bottom of door frames and exterior casing
- Deck board ends and the boards against the house
- The base of deck posts, where mulch and sprinklers keep them wet
- Stair stringers and the bottom step

## Rot or Termites? How to Tell

Push a screwdriver into the soft spot and look at what comes out. Rot looks dark, stringy or crumbly, and it often breaks into small cubes as it dries.

Termites leave something different. According to <a href="https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/biology-behavior-of-eastern-subterranean-termites">NC State Extension</a>, eastern subterranean termites live in every county in North Carolina, and mud inside the wood points to them, not just water.

If you see mud tubes or mud packed inside the wood, call a pest control company before anyone repairs the wood. Fixing the board first just gives the termites a new meal.

Mold and mildew on the surface are a different thing again. NC State notes that mold and mildew don't rot wood.

### Step 1: Epoxy

Epoxy is a two-part repair. First a thin consolidant soaks into the soft wood and hardens it. Then an epoxy filler rebuilds the missing shape, and it gets sanded and painted like wood.

It only works on dry wood. <a href="https://www.thisoldhouse.com/woodworking/how-to-fix-rotted-wood-with-epoxy">This Old House</a> warns that epoxy will not bond properly with moist wood, and <a href="https://uccoatings.com/blogs/brand-blogs/how-to-restore-rotted-wood-with-liquidwood-and-woodepox">Abatron</a> prefers moisture below 18%.

Epoxy is the right call for a soft corner on a sill, a rotted spot at the bottom of a door casing or a small hole in trim. It's the wrong call for anything that carries weight.

### Step 2: Patch in new wood

When a section is too far gone for filler, the rotten part gets cut out to clean wood and a new piece is glued and fastened into the gap. Carpenters call this a dutchman.

You keep the original piece, its profile and its paint lines, and you only pay for the part that failed. It works well on long sills, door frames and wide casing where the rot is at one end.

### Step 3: Replace the piece

Some pieces aren't worth saving. A deck board that's soft across its width, trim that crumbles end to end, or a sill rotted past the 10% line gets replaced.

Replacing one piece is still far smaller than replacing the window, the door or the deck. And it's the moment to upgrade to a material that can't rot.

## Window Sills: Repair the Sill, Not the Whole Window

The biggest fear we hear is being told a soft sill means a new window. Most of the time it doesn't. The sill is wood that sits under the window, and wood can be repaired or replaced on its own.

<a href="https://www.thisoldhouse.com/windows/how-to-repair-a-rotted-windowsill">This Old House</a> gives a clear line: if more than 10 percent of the sill is rotten, the old sill should be cut off and replaced. Below that, the bad wood comes out and gets filled or patched.

For a replacement sill, This Old House used cellular PVC, which doesn't rot. The material ran about $6.75 per foot plus a $74 adhesive kit, at national prices.

The window itself becomes the question only when the rot has moved past the sill into the frame and the opening around it. If that's the case, we tell you, and you'll need a window company for the unit. We repair the wood around the window; we don't sell or replace windows.

## Exterior Trim and Door Frames

Rot on trim usually starts at a joint: where the side casing meets the sill, at the bottom of a door frame, or anywhere caulk has cracked and let water in behind the paint.

Small spots get epoxy. A rotted bottom on a door frame or casing usually gets a new section spliced in, which keeps the rest of the frame and the door hanging where it is.

When a whole trim board is gone, it gets replaced. On the pieces that see the most water, PVC trim is worth asking about, because it takes paint like wood and doesn't rot.

## Deck Boards, Railings and Posts

On a deck, epoxy has a small role. A soft board gets replaced, because a deck board carries people. The good news is that replacing a few boards is a normal repair, not a new deck.

While the boards are up, someone should check the joists underneath. A soft board sometimes means the framing below needs attention too. Our [wood rot deck repair](/repairs/deck-repair) always includes that look.

Posts, beams and the ledger where the deck meets the house are structural. Those never get filled with epoxy. If rot has reached them, they get replaced, and that part gets priced as its own job.

Use the right lumber for the replacement. According to <a href="https://www.proremodeler.com/new-rules-pressure-treated-lumber">Pro Remodeler</a>, a revised treated-lumber standard may require ground-contact wood on deck ledgers, beams, joists, railing posts and railings, depending on how it is read.

New boards look different from weathered ones until the deck gets refinished. If you want the whole deck to match, [deck staining and sealing](/repairs/deck-staining-sealing) goes after the repair, not before.

## Fix the Water First

A repair over a wet spot fails again. Before anyone fills or replaces wood, find where the water comes from. In Charlotte, it's usually one of these:

- A clogged or overflowing gutter dumping water on a sill or a deck
- A downspout that empties against the house or the deck
- Sprinklers hitting the trim, posts or siding
- Mulch or soil piled against posts and door frames
- Cracked caulk or paint on a sill that lets water sit inside
- A roof or flashing leak above the trim

Some of those we handle as part of the repair, like caulk, paint and clearing mulch away from a post. A leaking roof or a failed gutter may need another trade, and we'll tell you so you know who to call.

## Where to Save and Where Not to Cut Corners

If you're going to save somewhere, save here:

- **Repair the sill instead of replacing the window**, when the rot hasn't reached the frame.
- **Use two-step epoxy instead of rigid filler** on small spots. It flexes with the wood and lasts longer.
- **Patch a section instead of replacing a long piece** when most of it is sound.
- **Replace only the soft deck boards**, not the whole deck surface.
- **Use PVC for replacement sills and trim** in spots that stay wet.

This is the part we don't cut corners on:

- **Stopping the water.** Without that, every repair is temporary.
- **Drying the wood** before epoxy goes on.
- **Removing everything soft.** Rot left behind keeps spreading under the filler.
- **Paint and seal, edges included.** The sealed edge is what keeps the repair dry.
- **No epoxy on posts, beams or ledgers.** Structural wood gets replaced.

## What to Ask Before You Sign an Estimate

Ask for every answer in writing:

1. **Which fix they propose** (epoxy, patch or replace) and why.
2. **What happens if the rot is bigger** once the wood is open, and at what price.
3. **What causes the water** and who fixes it.
4. **The replacement material:** treated lumber, ground-contact lumber, PVC, and the epoxy brand.
5. **Whether paint or sealing is included.**
6. **The labor warranty**, and what it covers.

If someone tells you a soft sill means a new window, ask them to show you how far the rot goes. A fair answer comes with a screwdriver test and photos.
