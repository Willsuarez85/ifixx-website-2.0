A screened in porch in Charlotte can cost **a few thousand dollars or more than $40,000**. The range is that wide because the same words cover three different jobs: screens on a porch that already has a roof, a deck converted into a porch, or a new porch built from the footings up.

National guides put adding screens to an existing porch at **$2,000 to $4,000**. One of the best-known porch builders in Charlotte says its porch projects <a href="https://www.archadeck.com/charlotte/what-we-build/screened-porches/" rel="nofollow">"generally start around $40k"</a>. Both numbers are real. They describe different work.

Below: the three paths side by side, how to know if your deck can carry a roof, where to save, what never gets cut, what Mecklenburg County asks for, and what to get in writing before you sign.

## How Much Does a Screened In Porch Cost in Charlotte?

Start with one question: **is there already a roof over the space?** The answer puts you on one of three paths, and they barely overlap in price.

| Path | Market price | What you're paying for | Best when |
|------|--------------|------------------------|-----------|
| Screens on a porch that already has a roof | $2,000 to $4,000 (national, "existing porch") | Framing for the screen, the screen and a door | The roof and floor are sound |
| Converting a deck you already have | No published range. Priced after a structure check | New roof, posts and beams, screen, ceiling | The deck passes the check |
| New screened porch from the footings up | $25 to $120 per sq ft (national). One Charlotte builder: from about $40k | Everything: footings, floor, roof, screen, ceiling | No deck, or the deck can't carry a roof |

The national figures come from [HomeLight's 2026 roundup](https://www.homelight.com/blog/cost-to-add-a-screened-in-porch/) of Angi and HomeAdvisor pricing. They are not Charlotte prices, and they are not ours. Use them to tell which path a quote belongs to.

One more reference for the first path: [Lawn Love's 2026 guide](https://lawnlove.com/blog/screened-in-porch-cost/) puts screening an existing 200-square-foot porch at about **$2,085** on average, with most people paying $1,680 to $2,490. Again, that is a national number.

## Why Quotes Run From $3,000 to $40,000

Here's where the money actually goes. The screen itself is the cheap part. Lawn Love lists fiberglass screen at **$0.15 per square foot** and aluminum at $0.30. On any porch, that line barely moves the total.

The roof is what moves it. A new porch roof needs:

- **Footings and posts** sized to carry the roof, not only people and furniture.
- **Beams and rafters** framed to the right pitch.
- **A tie-in to the house**, with flashing that sends water away from the wall.
- **Roofing** to match the house, plus a finished ceiling underneath.

That is why a quote for "screening in a porch" and a quote for "building a screened porch" can sit $30,000 apart and both be fair. Before you compare two numbers, check that they describe the same path.

## Can You Screen In the Deck You Already Have?

Sometimes, and when you can, it is usually the best value on the table. You keep a floor you already paid for and spend the money on the roof and the screen.

The catch is the structure. A deck framed for foot traffic is not automatically framed for a roof. Before anything else, we check three things:

- **Footings:** are they deep and wide enough for the added load?
- **Posts:** are they sound, and do they line up with where the roof posts need to land?
- **Joists and connections:** is the frame solid, and is the attachment to the house in good shape?

If the deck passes, converting it costs far less than building new. If it doesn't, we'd rather tell you at the estimate than after a roof is sitting on it. A deck that needs heavy reframing is often not much cheaper than starting from the ground.

## Where to Save Without Regretting It

If you're going to save somewhere, save here. None of these choices shortens the life of the porch.

- **Use what you already have.** A sound roof or a sound deck is the biggest saving available. Have it checked before you assume it has to go.
- **Pick fiberglass screen.** It is the lowest-priced screen on Lawn Love's list.
- **Match the house with asphalt shingles.** [Inch Calculator's cost guide](https://www.inchcalculator.com/screen-porch-cost-guide/) lists shingles at $160 to $300 per 100 square feet and metal roofing at $1,400 to $2,200. Those are national figures from an older update, so read them as a ratio.
- **Keep the finishes simple.** A painted ceiling and a floor you can hose off do the job. Exotic hardwoods under a roof are money most people never notice.
- **Build in the cold months.** Lawn Love notes that contractors may have more room in the schedule in slower seasons. A porch started in winter is ready when the mosquitoes come back.
- **Frame for the fan now.** Blocking and a box cost very little during the build. Cutting into a finished ceiling later costs a lot more.

## Where Not to Cut Corners

This is the part we don't cut corners on, and you shouldn't let anyone else do it either.

- **Footings, posts and beams.** They carry the roof for the life of the house. Nobody sees them, which is exactly why they get skipped.
- **The roof tie-in.** Pitch, flashing and the connection to the house decide whether the porch stays dry. A leak where the porch meets the wall damages the house, not only the porch.
- **The permit and inspections.** An inspector looking at the footings and the framing is cheap insurance on the most important parts of the job.
- **Pet screen on the lower panels**, if you have a dog that leans or scratches. It holds up where standard screen tears.

The expensive mistakes we'd warn you about:

- Putting a roof on a deck nobody checked.
- Comparing a "screen only" quote to a "new porch" quote as if they were the same job.
- Signing because the discount ends today. A fair price is still fair next week.
- Ordering materials before the HOA has answered.
- Closing the ceiling before the fan wiring is in.

## Do You Need a Permit for a Screened Porch in Charlotte?

If your project adds a roof or load-bearing structure, yes. Here is the rule in plain words, with the sources.

**State law.** [North Carolina G.S. 160D-1110(c)](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_160D/GS_160D-1110.html) says no building permit is required for work costing $40,000 or less in a single-family residence, unless the work involves certain things. Three of them matter for a porch:

- The addition, repair or replacement of **load-bearing structures**.
- **"The addition of roofing, excluding replacement."**
- Adding or changing **wiring**, other than like-kind replacement of devices and lighting fixtures.

So a porch with a new roof needs a permit whatever it costs. The same law says replacing pickets, railings, stair treads and decking on porches and decks does not need one, as long as the job stays within that $40,000 limit.

**Screens only.** Screening a porch that already has a roof, with no structural work and no new wiring, may fall inside that $40,000 exemption. The statute does not mention screens by name, so confirm with the county before work starts.

**Mecklenburg County.** [Code Enforcement's permitting page](https://code.mecknc.gov/permitting) repeats the same exceptions and adds that separate permits are required for each trade. Permits are grouped by project.

Zoning and setbacks are a separate question from the building permit. Your city or town sets them, so confirm how close to the property line you can build before the plans are final.

**Outside Mecklenburg.** If your home is in Union County, or across the state line in York or Lancaster County, a different office sets the rules and the process. Confirm with that office before you start.

### Who pulls the permit?

Ask this of every contractor, and get the answer in writing. Mecklenburg County's [homeowner permit page](https://code.mecknc.gov/permitting/hip) is direct about it: "Homeowners who are hiring contractors to do work for them should not use homeowner permits."

A homeowner permit is for an owner who lives in the house and does the work personally. If a contractor asks you to pull the permit in your name for work they will do, stop and ask why.

On our porch builds, permits and inspections are handled as part of the project, and we tell you up front what your build requires.

## HOA Approval: Ask Before You Order Materials

Many neighborhoods across [South Charlotte](/service-areas/south-charlotte) have an association with rules on roof style, colors and how far a structure can extend. This is a private process, separate from the county permit.

The practical advice is simple. If you have an HOA, read your covenants and send the request first. A porch redesigned after the lumber is delivered costs you twice.

Ask your contractor for a simple drawing and a material list you can attach to the request.

## How Long It Takes: Three Clocks

People ask how long the build takes. The better question is how long until you're sitting on it, and that depends on three clocks that run one after another.

| Clock | Who runs it | What to expect |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| HOA approval | Your association | No public timeline. It depends on your community, so start here |
| Permit | The county | When plan review applies, the county's goal for houses is an average of seven days from acceptance |
| Build | Your contractor | Most of our screened porch builds run a few weeks on site |

The county figure comes from Mecklenburg's [residential plan review page](https://code.mecknc.gov/plan-review/residential), which says its goal is to review plans for one and two-family dwellings "within an average of seven days" and that timeframes vary with volume. Ask whether your porch goes through plan review.

On the build clock, the roof tie-in and whether you start from an existing deck make the difference. You get a schedule with the estimate. Ask for the permit time and the on-site time as two separate lines.

## Fans and Lights: Plan the Wiring Early

A fan is what makes a porch usable in August, so decide on it before the ceiling goes up. Here is how the work splits.

- **Our part:** we frame the blocking and set the boxes during the build, so nobody cuts into a finished ceiling later.
- **Not our part:** wiring for a fan or lights is a separate trade and a separate permit. That work goes to the person you hire for it, and we coordinate our schedule with theirs.

Line that person up early. If the wiring goes in before the ceiling is closed, the job is clean and quick. If it comes after, it gets harder and more expensive.

## Screened Porch or Three-Season Room?

A screened porch keeps bugs out and lets all the air through. A three-season room swaps the screens for vinyl window panels that slide open, so you can close it against pollen, rain and cold snaps.

It costs more than screen, and many porches can be upgraded later. If you think you'll want it, say so at the estimate so the openings are framed with that in mind.

One permit note: Mecklenburg's plan review page lists "conversions of existing decks and screen porches into a sunroom or other enclosed rooms" among the projects that need plan review. Enclosing a porch is a different project in the county's eyes.

## What to Get in Writing Before You Sign

Ask for it in writing. A good estimate answers every one of these without you having to push.

- **Which path is this price for?** Screens only, a deck conversion, or a new build.
- **What did the structure check find?** Footings, posts and joists of the existing deck, in plain words.
- **How does the roof tie into the house?** Pitch, flashing and where it connects.
- **Who pulls the permit?** And in whose name.
- **What happens if you find rotted wood?** How it is priced and who approves it before the work continues.
- **What screen, ceiling and flooring are included?** By name, not "standard."
- **Who handles the fan wiring, and when?**
- **How many weeks on site, and how long for the permit?** As two separate numbers.
- **Has the HOA approved it?** Before any material is ordered.

A contractor who can't answer these hasn't finished the estimate. Wait for the answers before you sign.
