Ceramic and porcelain tile cost about **$10 to $50 per square foot** nationally, before labor. But the tile itself is the cheap part. Most of the money, and most of the risk, goes into what sits behind it: the membrane, the mortar and a flat, sloped base.

So the smart move for a tight budget is simple. Buy mid-range tile and spend on what you can't see. And if your shower already has tile, check whether it only needs new grout before you pay for a full redo.

Below you'll find what tile costs by material, what to buy for each surface, the layers we never cut, where you can save, and how to tell a grout repair from a real problem.

## Porcelain vs. Ceramic vs. Natural Stone: What to Buy for Each Surface

You don't need one tile for the whole room. Each surface asks for something different, and matching the tile to the job is where you save without losing quality.

| Surface | What it needs | Smart pick |
|---------|---------------|------------|
| **Walls outside the shower** | Looks, easy cleaning | Ceramic, the budget winner |
| **Shower walls** | Water resistance, few weak spots | Ceramic or porcelain over a membrane |
| **Shower floor** | Grip when wet, follows the slope | Small porcelain tile or mosaic |
| **Bathroom floor** | Strength, grip, flat install | Porcelain, standard or large format |

**Porcelain** is denser than ceramic and absorbs less water, so it handles floors and heavy use better. It also comes in looks that pass for marble or stone at a lower price and with less upkeep.

**Ceramic** is softer and cheaper. On a wall that never sees standing water, it does the job for years. This is the easiest place in the room to save.

**Natural stone** looks great and asks for more. It costs more to buy and install, needs sealing, and polished stone gets slippery. If you love it, use it on one wall or a vanity backsplash.

### What to look for on the box

On the shower floor, ask for the tile's **wet DCOF rating**, which measures slip resistance. According to [Crossville](https://www.crossville.com/learn/porcelain-tile-dcof-ratings-and-applications/), wet interior floors call for a wet DCOF of **0.42 or greater**, and shower floors fall in a higher category for standing water.

If the box or spec sheet doesn't list it, ask before you buy. A tile that looks right in the store can be the wrong tile for a wet floor.

## Large Format vs. Mosaic: Fewer Grout Lines or Better Grip?

Large tiles mean fewer grout lines, which means less to scrub. Small tiles mean more grout lines, which means more grip. The right answer depends on the surface.

- **Large format on walls:** fewer lines, a cleaner look, and less grout to keep clean.
- **Large format on the bathroom floor:** works well, but only on a floor that is truly flat.
- **Mosaic on the shower floor:** follows the slope to the drain and gives your feet more grip.
- **Mosaic everywhere:** costs more. Fixr says mosaic runs about 10% more to install, and This Old House notes that intricate patterns like herringbone need more cutting and time.

The catch with big tiles is the floor under them. According to [TileLetter](https://www.tileletter.com/large-format-tile-know-your-numbers-for-installation-success/), for tiles longer than 15 inches on one side, the surface can't vary more than **1/8 inch in 10 feet**. An uneven floor makes edges stick up and tiles crack.

So if you want large tile on an older floor, budget for leveling first. Our [tile floor installation](/remodeling/flooring-installation) always starts with checking the floor.

## What Goes Behind the Tile (and Why We Never Cut It)

This is the part we don't cut corners on. A shower can look perfect on day one and still be failing behind the tile. These layers are what keep water out of your walls.

- **A waterproof membrane in the wet area.** Cement board resists water but is not waterproof. The tile industry handbook, quoted by [DIYTileGuy](https://www.diytileguy.com/durock-cement-board-waterproof/), says a waterproof membrane or vapor retarder must be specified.
- **Full mortar coverage.** TileLetter cites **95% coverage** in wet areas and 80% elsewhere. Gaps under a tile leave room for water and cracks.
- **A real slope to the drain** on the shower floor, so water never sits.
- **A flat floor** under large tile, as above.
- **Cure time.** Fixr recommends waiting at least three days before using a new shower.

Ask any quote to name the membrane brand and say how the shower gets tested before tile goes on. If it isn't in writing, it may not be in the price.

## Epoxy vs. Cement Grout: Where Each One Makes Sense

Grout is cheap to choose well and expensive to fix later. According to [Fine Homebuilding](https://www.finehomebuilding.com/project-guides/tiling/whats-the-difference-epoxy-vs-cement-grout), cement grout joints need sealer and epoxy joints don't.

| | Cement grout | Epoxy grout |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost to regrout** | $5 to $7 per sq ft | $8 to $12 per sq ft |
| **Sealing** | Needs sealer | No sealer needed |
| **Stains** | Can stain over time | Resists stains |
| **Best place** | Walls that dry out | Shower floor and lower walls |

Regrout prices come from [This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/bathrooms/cost-to-regrout-shower) and are national.

A good mix for a tight budget: epoxy where water sits, cement grout on the dry walls. You pay for epoxy only where it earns its cost.

## Where You Can Save Without Losing Quality

If you're going to save somewhere, save here.

- **Mid-range porcelain or ceramic instead of stone.** Porcelain that looks like marble gives you the look for less, with no sealing.
- **Standard sizes and simple layouts on the walls.** Straight or stacked layouts take less cutting than herringbone or mosaic.
- **One detail you'll see every day.** A niche or a single accent strip reads better than mosaic across the whole shower.
- **Cement grout on dry walls.** Save the epoxy for the floor.
- **Keep the valve and drain where they are.** If they have to move, that pipe work is done by the right trade, and it adds cost and days. We coordinate it so you don't have to.
- **Repair before you replace.** If the tile is solid, new grout may be all your shower needs. More on that below.

## Expensive Mistakes to Avoid

These are the choices that turn a tile job into a bigger repair a few years later.

1. **Skipping the membrane** because the wall is "cement board anyway."
2. **Large tile on an uneven floor** without leveling first.
3. **Polished stone or glass on the shower floor.** This Old House warns that both get slippery.
4. **New grout over old grout.** Fixr is direct about it: this causes cracking. The old grout has to come out.
5. **Rushing the cure** so the shower can be used sooner.
6. **Changing the tile after work starts.** A late swap means new cuts, new trim and lost days.

## Shower Tile Repair or Full Redo?

A lot of showers that look tired don't need a full redo. The symptom tells you which job you're facing. Prices below are national.

| What you see | What it usually needs | Typical cost |
|--------------|----------------------|--------------|
| Stained grout, no cracks | Color sealing | From about $500 per shower (Fixr) |
| Cracked or missing grout, tile is solid | Regrout: old grout out, new grout in | $560 to $880 (Fixr); $600 to $2,500 (This Old House) |
| One or two cracked tiles | Replace those tiles | Depends on matching tile; free estimate from photos |
| Hollow-sounding tile, soft wall, loose handle, musty smell | Open the wall and check the membrane and backing | Retiling a shower averages $3,504 (This Old House) |

How do you check for water behind the tile? [This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/bathrooms/saving-a-soggy-shower-wall) suggests tapping the tiles gently with a rubber mallet. A hollow sound means the tile has lost its bond.

Other warning signs: grout that stays dark, peeling paint on the wall next to the shower, and spots that give when you press them. If you see those, regrout won't fix it. The problem is behind the tile.

According to [Fixr](https://www.fixr.com/costs/shower-regrouting), regrouting a shower averages **$700**. That's a small fraction of a redo, which is why it's worth checking first.
